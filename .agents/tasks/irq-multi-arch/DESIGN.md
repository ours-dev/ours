# irq-multi-arch: one interrupt line layer for x86, riscv and arm, with cascaded controllers in user space

**Date**: 2026-11-01
**Status**: Proposed

## Problem

`kernel/irq` was built for one machine: x86 with an IO-APIC. On 2026-11-01 the user said that riscv is being introduced on other branches now (`origin/feat/riscv`, tip `9b8e7690`, and five slices of it), and that arm and GPIO controllers follow soon. The same day the user asked whether, if every device driver except the root interrupt controller and MMIO lives in user space, `kernel/irq` still has a value or should be removed. Two read-only reviews by agents (13 in all; one re-checked its claims against Linux's irqchip sources; the transcripts are in the session, not in the tree) found:

- The layer is not a device-driver layer. It is the root controller's line layer: 14 files and 2168 lines (1016 of code), with two production owners of a line, the legacy timer (a Permanent action) and the user-space interrupt object. More drivers in user space make the interrupt object its main customer. The cascaded GPIO mapping the user asked about, a leaf's number behind a root line, is not in it: it has one chip, one linear domain and no parent.
- Removing it would move five duties, not delete them: a per-GSI slot that outlives every object, the detach barrier under the line lock L1, the level hold until the ack, attach with the mode in one step, and hwirq to virq for the user ABI. They would go into `platform/pc` with the same lock order, the roughly 400 hard lines of `desc.cpp`, and a rewrite of 2938 lines of kernel-shell tests, and it would reverse genirq decisions of 2026-10-05 (irq-genirq D1, D2, D8). A thin per-vector slot re-derives the same five duties and leaves the level mask with two writers.
- What the new controllers need is small, and mostly at the edges of the layer: the platform entry seam is x86-shaped, and `set_affinity` is a stub that answers Ok. This is the reviews' reading and the user's to confirm (D0).

The riscv side has its own plan. These documents exist only on `origin/feat/riscv` (read them with `git show origin/feat/riscv:<path>`): `.agents/tasks/riscv-kernel/PLAN.md` step 2 writes the kernel's trap vector and riscv's `IrqFrame`, step 5 "a PLIC `irq::Chip`", step 10 sends IPIs through SBI; `.agents/tasks/arch-platform-seams/DESIGN.md` has the interrupt entry as an open decision (P-irq, refactor item R19, issue I14) and says it must be decided before riscv step 2, because a PLIC gives the number only when the hart claims it. riscv is between its steps 1a (landed) and 1b (not started), so the deadline is near. Those branches have no `irq::Domain` implementation (`domain.hpp` is empty there), no `interrupt_map` and no acpi-route, since they were cut before the interrupt-system work of `.agents/tasks/interrupt-system/`. This design is the contract the two lines of work meet at.

## Goals and non-goals

Goals:
- One line layer that carries the IO-APIC, GIC SPIs and the PLIC with the five duties kept in one place. An APLIC is a design constraint, not a deliverable: no riscv document plans one, and the riscv verify boot is plain `-machine virt`.
- The kernel and user-space split of interrupt controllers stated as a test, so that a controller nobody has met yet is placed by it, and not by a list.
- Cascaded controllers (a GPIO bank, a mux, an expander) need no kernel code beyond one signal on the virtual interrupt.
- The riscv branch writes its PLIC chip and its entry against a stated contract, and the two branches do not edit the same seam twice.

Non-goals:
- MSI controllers (ITS, IMSIC, APLIC in MSI mode). Only: nothing here may make them a rewrite.
- GICv5 and extended SPI numbering. The first arm cut refuses them.
- Per-CPU interrupts inside the line layer (SGI, PPI, the hart's local causes, the timers and IPIs).
- Deleting or reshaping the pieces that serve no new controller: `Shared`, `issue_ack`, `startup`, `dump`, `Data::platform_data` and the x86 local APIC chip are uncalled or test-only and stay as scaffolding under the hands-off rule. `Permanent` is not among them: the legacy timer's line is attached with it.
- Any non-x86 controller code, a device-tree route service, the Driver Manager, performance work.

## Design

### Where the kernel ends and user space begins

Proposed (D1). A controller belongs to the kernel if either of these holds:

- K1. The line's flow or the CPU's own path touches it in interrupt context or while a CPU comes up: claim or acknowledge, end of interrupt, the mask and unmask the flow does, threshold, per-hart enable, the CPU's own registers.
- K2. It allocates or aims a delivery resource that is shared across protection domains: vectors, LPIs, EIIDs, an ITS device table, the routing of a distributor, an APLIC target.

K1 and K2 restate the three reasons of interrupt-system's Responsibilities table: privilege and interrupt context are K1, isolation is K2. By them the IO-APIC, the GIC distributor, redistributor and CPU interface, the ITS, the PLIC, an APLIC and an IMSIC are the kernel's. A controller that is only MMIO behind a root line is user space's: a GPIO bank, an expander on I2C or SPI, and an interrupt router whose output is a root line. Two cautions, both for D1. A router's MMIO can raise any root line it reaches, kernel-owned ones included; that is the power an MMIO resource already grants, bounded by the kernel's MMIO deny list (a table of up to 32 page-granular ranges, a few on a PC, which fails closed beyond that). And lines the kernel itself consumes (the console UART, an SMMU, a timer frame) are reachable by number from firmware tables, so `interrupt_map` has to answer for them (D4b).

The user's own rule of 2026-10-30, recorded on `origin/feat/riscv` in `.claude/skills/ours-kernel-design/references/layering.md`, puts the local APIC and the riscv hart's timer, IPIs and start and stop in `kernel/arch`, every other chip (IO-APIC, HPET, PLIC) in `kernel/driver/<class>/<chip>`, and the board in `kernel/platform/<board>`. K1 and K2 say which of those chips are the kernel's at all; the rule says where they live. A PLIC chip is written in `kernel/driver/<class>/plic`; `io_apic.cpp` is still in `kernel/arch/x86/apic` and is due to move there too, so an edit of it in step 3 should land before that move or be redone after it.

### The interrupt entry

The three architectures find the interrupt differently, and none but x86 does it by arithmetic on a vector:

| Architecture | How the number is found | What ends it |
|---|---|---|
| x86 | The vector in the trap frame, less the platform's base | The local APIC EOI |
| arm GICv2 and GICv3 | A read of the acknowledge register, which also takes the interrupt | A write of the same value back; on v3 the acknowledging CPU interface |
| riscv PLIC | A read of this hart's claim register | A write of the id back to the same register |

Events of the entry (D3 (c), chosen by the user on 2026-11-01). Objects first: arch's trap entry; the root controller's handler, a function the controller's driver installs with `irq::set_root_handler()` before the first interrupt can arrive (the platform code that finds the chip and starts its driver does the installing); the line layer; the architecture's own handlers for per-CPU interrupts (timer, IPI).

1. The trap enters arch, which calls `irq::handle_irq_preamble(frame)` once for the trap.
2. Arch first separates the CPU's own causes. On x86 that is the vector switch (`x86_dispatch_exception()` in `faults.cpp`: the timer, error, spurious and IPI vectors); on riscv `scause`, whose software and timer causes are arch's. Only the external interrupt goes on. On arm only the controller's own acknowledge read can tell, since it also returns SGIs and PPIs (INTID below 32), so the root handler hands those back to arch's handler.
3. Arch calls `irq::handle_root_irq(frame)`, which calls the installed handler. There is exactly one root handler per kernel; calling it before one is installed is a kernel bug and stops. The handler identifies the interrupt (the vector, an acknowledge read, a claim). It takes one interrupt per trap in the first cut. Looping while the controller has more is a throughput option, with no bound and a longer preemption-disabled window; it is put to the user with a measurement when it matters (`docs/repairs`).
4. The handler drops what is not an interrupt (GIC ids 1020 to 1023, a claim of 0).
5. For the rest it calls `irq::handle_irq_generic(hwirq)`, which answers whether a line ended the interrupt.
6. If no line had it, the handler ends it itself: the GIC deactivates it, a PLIC must complete it, the x86 handler sends the local APIC EOI. The reference PLIC handler in Linux only warns and leaves the source wedged in the gateway, which is the case this clause is for.
7. Arch calls `irq::handle_irq_postamble()` and takes the preemption decision.

The user chose (c) over the recommended (a), the numberless `platform_handle_irq(frame)`. Under (c) the platform header has no IRQ function: `kernel/platform/include/ours/platform/interrupt.hpp` goes, and with it pc's three forwarders. The no-line EOI moves from arch to the root handler, which changes a recorded genirq detail ("arch sends it only for a vector with no line", `.claude/skills/ours-kernel-design/references/task.md`, the bullet "Each line runs a flow handler"); step 2 (`13a1cc09`) did both. The registration and the call live in `kernel/irq` (the generic kernel defines them; a driver implements the handler), so they carry no `arch_` or `platform_` prefix, which the user's naming rule of 2026-10-30 reserves for functions a layer defines for the generic kernel. The frame stays `arch::IrqFrame` through the dispatcher `<arch/interrupt.hpp>`: no second neutral type. `origin/feat/riscv` already made the include edit in the platform header (riscv-kernel step 1a.1, `c0753c85`); under (c) that header goes, and whichever branch lands second drops the edit. Refactor item R19 of arch-platform-seams is written for (a); under (c) its files are the same and its header goes.

Counterparts. Linux, the chosen form: `set_handle_irq`, which a driver calls so that arch's entry reaches it. On arm64 that is `gic_handle_irq()` of `irq-gic-v3.c`. On riscv the registered root is the per-hart interrupt controller's `riscv_intc_irq()`, and the PLIC's `plic_handle_irq()` is chained under it by `irq_set_chained_handler()`; ours keeps the cause dispatch in arch and puts the PLIC's claim in the root handler. All read from Linux master on 2026-11-01 by a review agent, not kept in the tree. The flow is `handle_fasteoi_irq`, and the rejected cascade is `irq_set_chained_handler_and_data` with hierarchical domains. Zircon, the form not chosen: `platform_irq(frame)` with `int_handler_start` and `int_handler_finish` called by arch, as the seams document records them (`exceptions_c.cc:541`, `dev/pdev/interrupt/interrupt.rs:526`; cited there, not re-read).

### The chip contract

Clauses the chips of all architectures keep (step 3 wrote them into `chip.hpp`, in the comment of `Chip`; what follows is the design's reading, and `chip.hpp` is the text):

- `mask`, `unmask` and `issue_eoi` may run in interrupt context or in thread context with interrupts off. `mask` and `unmask` are called under L1; `issue_eoi` is called in interrupt context, under L1 unless the line is Permanent. None sleeps, and none waits on another CPU. A bounded poll (the GICv3 redistributor's write-pending bit) is fine.
- `issue_eoi` runs on the CPU that took the interrupt, inside `handle_irq`. It is never deferred to `finalize_oneshot` or to a thread.
- A controller whose mask completes on another CPU (an IMSIC) cannot hold a Oneshot line. `check_oneshot` cannot tell: it only tests that the `mask` and `unmask` pointers exist. Step 3 chose a rule, no flag: such a chip leaves `unmask` null, which refuses a Oneshot action and `irq::unmask()` too, so it serves no owner until a flag exists (MSI's to add).
- `configure` refuses with NotSupported a mode the controller cannot do (a GIC SPI takes level-high and edge-rising only; the PLIC cannot invert polarity), and leaves the source masked on success and as it was on a refusal. A controller with no mode register accepts the mode it is told, the firmware's, which it cannot check, and leaves `trigger_mode` null: the line takes the flow of the mode told, and the attach with no mode refuses a Oneshot action. The first reading, that such a chip records the mode and answers it from `trigger_mode`, was dropped by the independent review of step 3: a chip that answers never meets the refusal, and the recording needs a table the layer does not need. The route service gives the mode that firmware gives, so non-x86 platforms always create an interrupt with an explicit mode.
- A chip with several instances finds its instance from `hirqnum`, as the IO-APIC does.
- `set_affinity` answers NotSupported until it is real; its signature is settled with the real implementation (interrupt-system P4).
- `issue_eoi` is called on a source `mask` has masked (an interrupt on a line nobody is on, a Oneshot interrupt held for its owner): a controller that ignores the end of interrupt of a masked source masks some other way. In `chip.hpp`.
- For the PLIC chip's author, not in `chip.hpp`: mask with the source's priority register (0 and 1) and keep the enable bits set, so a completion for a masked source is still valid.

What a controller driver provides, which a PLIC author needs and the per-line operations do not cover: the initial state of every source (priority 0, so masked; the enable bit for the boot hart's context, since the boot CPU takes every device interrupt; the threshold), a bring-up and bring-down hook per CPU (`kernel/include/ours/cpu_hotplug_hook.hpp`), its MMIO reserved with `platform::reserve_kernel_mmio` (`kernel/platform/include/ours/platform/mmio.hpp:30`), and the use of `hirqnum` alone: `virqnum` never equals it. D10 (b) says otherwise (2026-11-01, not built): a controller registers its numbers as a domain of its own. Until it is built, a controller registers all its numbers through one `irq::init_lines` call at boot.

### Numbering

(Superseded in part by D10 (b), D12 and D13, 2026-11-01, not built: a hardware number is read within a domain, and on x86 the one domain keeps the GSI as its number.) The hardware numbers of a machine are one platform-wide space: a controller owns `[base, base + n)`, as ACPI's `gsi_base` does for a PLIC or an APLIC, and a GIC's number is its INTID (ACPI on arm calls the INTID the GSI). The interrupt path is reached through the root handler, so nothing in the public interface should promise an array indexed by hardware number; that keeps MSI from being a rewrite. One table built once per machine (D10 (a)) keeps what interrupt-system and `task.md` record: the table is read on the interrupt path without a lock, it is indexed by hardware number, and every virq is above every hardware number of the machine. A table per controller, a directory from virq range to table and registration after boot (D10 (b)) would depart from all three and would need the first virq to come from one global allocator, since `Domain::init` starts each domain at the first multiple of 32 past its own highest number, so two domains would overlap in virq space. It is MSI's (P3), unless a machine needs it earlier.

### Cascades in user space

Objects: the parent, a physical interrupt on a root line, level, Oneshot; the GPIO driver G, which holds the parent and the bank's MMIO; one virtual interrupt per pin, made by G; a client K, which holds one pin's interrupt and does not know it is virtual; the root controller's flow in the kernel.

Events for a level child on a level parent:

1. Pin i asserts, so the parent line asserts.
2. The kernel's flow masks the parent, runs the action, and holds the line masked until G acks (the interrupt object's action is Oneshot and always answers WakeThread).
3. G wakes, reads the bank's status, masks pin i in the bank's own register, and calls `interrupt_trigger` on pin i's virtual interrupt.
4. G acks the parent, and the kernel unmasks the line. Pin i is masked in the bank, so the parent deasserts.
5. K wakes and clears the cause in its device.
6. K acks (the next `interrupt_wait`, or `interrupt_ack` on a port).
7. The kernel raises Idle on pin i's virtual interrupt. G, which waited for it with `object_wait_async` after the trigger, sees it, and unmasks pin i in the bank.

Step 6 is the gap. An edge child is fine, since G clears the latch when it forwards. Step 4 (D5) closed it with one signal on the virtual interrupt, `Signals::Idle`, bit 6 of `signals.hpp`: up while the object is owed no ack. It is up from the creation. `interrupt_trigger` drops it before the interrupt can be taken, so that the ack's raise is the later of the two; the base class raises it where it already calls `unmask_interrupt()` (from `wait()` and from `ack()`, with the object's spin lock dropped, in thread context), and `interrupt_destroy` raises it for good. What the tree showed, and how the step settled it:

- `update_state` takes a Mutex and cannot be called with the spin lock held, so the signal is set after the state change, with the lock dropped, at the places `unmask_interrupt()` is called from and at the end of `trigger()` and `destroy()`. A program cannot clear a bit, so the signal is a level, not an event: a waiter that comes after the ack finds it up.
- `unmask_interrupt()` is reached only when an ack clears the debt. If K closes its handle or exits without acking, nothing is destroyed while G holds its own handle, no signal comes, and pin i stays masked in G's bank. That is the safe state, and it stays. Nothing yet owns replacing the interrupt for that pin: `.agents/tasks/driver-manager/DESIGN.md` does not mention it (open question below). A destroy by K raises Idle, and G's next trigger answers Cancelled.
- The first version dropped Idle before delivering and raised it after the ack, and the independent review found that a trigger landing between an ack's state change and its raise left Idle up while an ack was owed. It is now set from the state after every change that moves the debt (`sync_signals()`), and again if the debt moved while it was being set, so it follows the state and lags it only while a call on the same interrupt is in progress. A destroyed object is not owed anything, so a trigger it refuses leaves Idle up.

The handle given to K is a `handle_duplicate` of G's with Wait, Read, Write and Transfer, and without Signal and Duplicate: `interrupt_wait` checks Wait, `interrupt_bind` Read, `interrupt_ack` Write, and `interrupt_trigger` Signal. `interrupt_destroy` asks for no right, so K can still destroy its pin's interrupt; that is accepted here, or it is a decision of its own. The alternative to the signal, a message from K to G, makes K tell the two kinds apart.

Nothing else in the kernel changes for GPIO. The three shapes seen in Linux (one parent source per pin, one summary line per bank with a status register, a mux with a fixed parent) all reduce to a parent from `interrupt_map` and `interrupt_create` plus a virtual interrupt per pin. A leaf pin has no kernel number.

### The user ABI

**Superseded by D4a (2026-11-01):** the user chose the specifier form now, so this paragraph's one number is the interface until step 12 of PLAN.md replaces it with `interrupt_map(resource, domain, hwirq, out virq)`. `interrupt_map(resource, source, out virq)` keeps its one number (interrupt-system D11 (a)). Its meaning becomes: the root controller's architectural number in the platform's single numbering. On arm that is the INTID for SPIs (ACPI's GSI as is, device-tree cells with 32 added by the route service). On riscv it is the PLIC source id (plus `gsi_base` under ACPI). SGIs, PPIs, the hart's local causes and the IMSIC's IPI id are never sources. Decoding device-tree cells is keyed by the controller's compatible string and lives in a user-space route service, which holds a read-only copy of the device tree as acpi-route holds the ACPI tables. Its answer must be tagged from the start, `Root{source, trigger, polarity}` or `Leaf{controller, cells}`, since a chain of `interrupts-extended` can end at a GPIO controller. That service is a later task.

Before step 5, `interrupt_map` answered Ok for a line the kernel holds (the legacy timer's), and `interrupt_create` answered AlreadyBound, the same code as for a line another program holds. Step 5 (D4b) made both answer AccessDenied for a kernel-owned line, so the route service can tell the kernel's line from one a program holds. The marker for "kernel-owned" in the line is Permanent (`irq::is_kernel_owned()`): a kernel driver that takes a line for its own uses a Permanent action. `interrupt_map` is asked with the root, so for its caller the AccessDenied it already had for "not the root" cannot be confused with the new one; a caller without the root gets the first answer and learns nothing about the line.

interrupt-system D11 recorded the specifier form (b) for "when the first non-x86 platform arrives". riscv is that platform. This design proposes to keep (a) anyway and narrow the trigger to a device-tree platform with two root controllers whose numbers collide and nobody owns the flattening. That departs from D11's wording and is the user's to decide (D4a).

### Reclaiming a removed domain

D16 lets a domain be removed after boot. The kernel has no RCU, epoch or quiescent-state code (a grep of `kernel/` and `system/lib` found none), so this section is the mechanism and the rule. It was written from three reads of the tree and an independent design, then reviewed by three reviewers (grace-period correctness, locks and contexts, removal semantics); two defects were blocking and several should-fix, all folded in below and listed at the end. The user told the assistant to proceed and not to ask for a written argument first (2026-11-01, "你直接按步骤做，别让我写论证"), which departs from the rule in CLAUDE.md that the user writes a concurrency argument first; the argument below is the assistant's, and the user may still grade it.

- **The mechanism (D18).** RCU whose readers run with interrupts off, and a grace period forced by one IPI rendezvous (`IpiService::sync_exec` on every online CPU). No epoch counter, no per-CPU quiescent-state report, no change to the scheduler or the idle loop. Why this flavour: the tree has no tick, an idle CPU sleeps until an IPI, a CPU running one thread never enters the scheduler (so there is nowhere to report a quiescent state), `scheduler.cpp` is ask-first and `fair.*` is hands-off, and an IPI cannot arrive inside an IF=0 section at all (the sampler's stop barrier already rests on that, `session.cpp`). The cost, said plainly: for the control-call readers alone, which are cold, a per-domain reference count or a sleeping lock would have served as well; only the grace period also covers the interrupt path, which may not take a lock or write a shared counter per interrupt. The user chose epoch/RCU (D16), so it is not substituted.
- **The read side.** `IrqRcuReadGuard` is RAII over `AutoInterruptDisabler`, and over `AutoPreemptDisabler` once a thread exists: the marker is the CPU's IF flag, it stores nothing, it nests, and it is legal when IF is already 0. Preemption is held off as well because interrupts off keep the CPU from taking the rendezvous but not a thread from being parked in the middle of its read: a read that wakes another thread asks for a reschedule, which is delivered when the preemption count next falls to zero, and the reader would be switched out with its read open (the independent review of step 8 found it; before the first thread, at boot, there is nothing to hold). So a read also never wakes a thread. One `irq::` facade call is one guard, from the directory load to the last use of the Desc, L1 and LI included, so nothing is used outside it; no control call sleeps, allocates or copies from user memory after the lookup. `interrupt_map` holds the guard over `virq_of(domain, hwirq)` and `is_kernel_owned` only and copies to user after it closes: the virq that escapes is a plain number, safe because virqs are never reused (D14). The interrupt path is a reader with no marker: the gate leaves IF=0, the root handler loads its `DomainSlot` (D15, an atomic table pointer) with acquire, and a null table makes `handle_irq_generic` answer false so the handler ends the interrupt itself. Thread-context entrants into the same code (`handle_irq_for_test`, any future software-injected interrupt) open a guard of their own around the lookup and the flow, and the slot load asserts IF is off.
- **The write side.** A registration needs no grace period: build the table, reserve the virq block from the high-water mark under a leaf spin lock LD (the writers' lock, never held with another), publish the slot (release), link the entry (release). A removal runs in thread context, IF on, preemption on, no spin lock held, not under `cpu_hotplug_lock`, not from a Dpc or a hotplug hook, and may block for as long as the slowest IF=0 section on any CPU: (1) under LD, Live to Removing (a second remover gets BadState; the id stays taken); (2) a read-only pass over every line (`is_kernel_owned` and the cancel hook) that refuses before anything is marked; (3) per line under L1, mark Dying (attach, unmask and configure answer NoResource, and the check comes before the chip is touched); (4) per line under L1, unlink every action, run its cancel hook, then store `m_virqnum = INVALID` with release as the last touch of the action, and mask the line; (5) retract the slot and unlink the entry under LD; (6) `irq_rcu_synchronize()`: take `cpu_hotplug_lock`, a full fence, `sync_exec` on the online mask with an empty task, re-read the mask and repeat for any CPU that appeared until it is stable, release; (7) free the Descs, the Domain arrays, the table and the entry, after asserting that every Desc's list is empty.
- **The grace-period argument.** Invariant G: a Desc, table or array of a removed domain is freed only after its pointer is unreachable from the list and the slot, and after every CPU has taken the `sync_exec` task since. Readers on every online CPU are in a guard or a handler, so IF=0 for as long as they hold a pointer; the task runs in the IPI handler, so only at an instant when that CPU has no open section. Every section on a CPU is therefore wholly before the task or wholly after it. Before: its loads precede the task's release in `answer()`, and the remover's acquire load of `outstanding` precedes the free. After: the retract is ordered before the task by the mailbox lock hand-off, so the section reads null or a newer list. The remover's own CPU runs its share inline with IF off and is not a reader. The three edges (publish with read, retract with the task, section end with the free) are explicit acquire and release pairs, none assumed from x86 or from `cli` and `sti`. The worst interleaving: a reader opens a guard on CPU1 and loads the entry and the table, CPU0 retracts and sends the IPI, CPU1 cannot take it while IF=0, finishes, closes the guard, takes the IPI, and only then does CPU0 see `outstanding` clear and free.
- **The removal rule (D19).** Refused with nothing changed: domain 0 (the root handler is installed once and cannot be uninstalled: NotSupported); a domain with a Permanent line, whose dispatch takes no L1 so nothing can wait for it (AlreadyBound); a domain with an action that has no cancel hook, so the owner opts in and is never silently disconnected (AlreadyBound); a second removal (BadState); an unknown id (NotFound); a boot on which an application processor gave up (BadState, tested as: every possible CPU is online or left by hotplug; dropped at step 8, see the review findings below). Cancelled in this order: all lines Dying first, then per line the actions unlinked and their hooks run under L1 in the handler's context. An interrupt object's hook sets it Destroyed under L2 and signals or takes the packet off the port, exactly the tail of `destroy()` minus the detach; an unbound waiter gets Cancelled. A bound port gets nothing (D20). Virtual interrupts have no line and are untouched; Irq ranges are arithmetic over virqs, so nothing is cancelled, and the dead block stays dead because the high-water mark never goes down (the narrowing call, not yet written, clips to the mark, not to live domains). Waited for: handlers in flight (L1 is the barrier for a non-Permanent line, the end-of-interrupt included) and every reader (the one grace period). A domain id is not a stable name for a controller, only a virq is: an id can be registered again after a removal, so a root program must not cache `(domain, hwirq)`.
- **What the owner must do before it calls `unregister_domain`.** The grace period covers readers of the table and the Descs, and nothing else. The slot must have static storage, or outlive the installation of whatever delivers into it. The registrant stops what delivers into the slot first (detach its action on the parent line, which is the L1 barrier there, and mask the parent) and frees the controller only after that. Phase 4 masks the domain's own lines and not the controller's upstream.
- **Making the interrupt object safe (the two blocking defects of the review).** The hook marks the object Destroyed while the remover still holds L1 and will still store into the action, which is embedded in the object. Two reviewers found the same use after free: the last handle close runs `destroy()` on another CPU, which finds the object already Destroyed and returns before it reaches the L1 barrier, so the object is freed under the remover. The rule that fixes it: `destroy()` always runs the barrier (it calls the detach on every call, including the Destroyed early return and the path from `on_zero_handles`) and accepts NotFound; seeing INVALID with acquire means the remover is finished with the action, and seeing a valid virq makes it queue on L1. The assert in the object's `unregister_interrupt_handler()` accepts Ok or NotFound. `Action::m_virqnum` becomes atomic and `virqnum()` and `is_attached()` are acquire loads.
- **As built (steps 8 to 10).** The directory is `irq::DomainRegistry` (the documents keep the older word): not a linked list but a fixed array of eight places, each an `IrqRcuPointer` to an immutable entry, so that a registration which fails or loses a race frees its table and entry at once, no reader having seen them. The writers keep each place's state and id under the one leaf lock and never read an entry; the entry is read by the remover that claimed its place and by readers. The application-processor precondition of D19 was not built (after the review of step 8 the fence and the sequentially consistent join carry that argument, and `irq_rcu_synchronize()` reads the online mask again), and neither were a fake chip that asserts the table is alive nor a CPU taken offline during a removal: the tests check the table's block and its destroyed flag instead.
- **As built (step 11).** The directory is the only path to a line. `init_lines()` is the root controller's registration: it registers Root and publishes the table into a `DomainSlot` the registrant owns, which on x86 is `platform/pc/interrupt.cpp`'s static `ROOT_SLOT`, and every control call is one `IrqRcuReadGuard` from the directory load to the last use of the line. The interrupt path keeps D15: the PC's handler loads its slot once (one acquire load) and hands the table to `handle_irq_generic()`, which answers false on a null table so the handler ends what it receives itself. The capacity and the scan were put to the user with the step and kept (both revisited at MSI); the before and after of the hot path is in the record, `docs/changes/261103/kernel-irq.md`.
- **Costs and what cannot be verified.** The read side is a flags save and restore and a short list scan per control call, not measured; only `finalize_oneshot` runs at ack rate. The list scan is O(domains) with IF=0; a sorted array published by pointer swap is the alternative, and the choice went to the user at the step that wired the facade (step 11, 2026-11-03): keep, with the scan and the eight places, both revisited at MSI. The grace period is one IPI to every online CPU (idle ones are woken; skipping the scheduler's Idling mask was rejected because that mask is a relaxed hint) and holds `cpu_hotplug_lock` for as long as the slowest IF=0 section, so no code that can run under that lock may wait for a removal. Not verifiable here: weak-memory machines, more than two CPUs, real IPI latency, a second real controller, a controller that vanishes, a non-x86 root handler, and lockdep (off by default and not linking when on). Nothing in the tree calls `unregister_domain` yet.
- **Review findings and what changed.** Blocking: the two above (one defect, found by two reviewers). Should-fix, all adopted: a late application processor or a hotplug transition without the lock could join after the mask was read (the fence, the re-read of the mask, and the BadState test); thread-context entrants such as `handle_irq_for_test` are readers too (they open a guard); a refused removal was not "nothing changed" (the read-only pass first, and NoResource from an attach while a removal is in progress is documented as possibly transient); an action without a hook was silently unlinked (it now makes the domain unremovable); a bound port is told nothing (D20); the high-water mark must be `max(mark, first + count)` of the block as `Domain::init` built it, with a test of a sparse domain removed and another registered after it; domain ids are reused. Notes adopted: the contexts `unregister_domain` may not be called from are written into its contract with assertions at entry, the entry and table are allocated before LD is taken, and the wait logs once if it passes a bound (dropped at step 8: `IpiService::sync_exec()` spins with no bound and no hook to log from, and the lockup detector already reports a CPU stuck with interrupts off).

### Hardware risks

- The Oneshot level flow masks a line after the root handler's claim and before the end of interrupt. On a PLIC that is a mask while a claim is open. Linux does it; seL4's PLIC code warns that masking during an open claim misbehaved on some hardware and so completes at the ack instead (both read on 2026-11-01, not kept in the tree). QEMU will not show it, so it is a checklist item for real silicon. If it fails, the hold becomes a chip operation, and the completion has to reach the claiming hart (D6).
- Every GICv3 mask polls the redistributor's write-pending bit in interrupt context. Any change to that goes through a performance report first.

## Alternatives considered

Each is "not recommended" with its decision row; none is the user's rejection.

- **Remove `kernel/irq` and fold it into `platform/pc`** (D0). It is a move, not a deletion (above), it loses what arm and riscv would reuse, and it reverses decisions of 2026-10-05.
- **Shrink it in place now** (D0): Domain to a fixed offset, Flow to a bit, a single action slot; about a quarter of the code. The reviews argued it when riscv and arm looked like placeholders. With the second and third controllers coming, the Chip table, the Domain and the flows have users. `Shared`, `issue_ack`, `startup` and `dump` are left as they are for the hands-off rule, not because they are used.
- **A thin per-vector slot with the mask a property of the object kind** (D0). The five duties come back, and the level mask has two writers again (irq-genirq D1).
- **Per-CPU interrupts as lines in `Desc`** (D2). Every CPU would take one global lock per tick, or the layer would need a lockless permanent path plus a per-CPU enable that `Data`, which carries no CPU, cannot express. They also cannot be held by a user program.
- **Cascaded controllers in the kernel**, as Linux's chained handlers and hierarchical domains (D1). A leaf's mask register is user-owned MMIO that interrupt context under L1 cannot reach, and the leaf needs no kernel number.
- **The ack of a level child as a message from the client to the controller driver** (D5). The client must not tell a virtual interrupt from a physical one.
- **A firmware-neutral specifier in `interrupt_map` now** (D4a). Deferred: one number is enough while a platform has one numbering.
- **The mode of a line owned by `Desc`** rather than by the controller. Not needed: the point above about `configure`. Revisit with the PLIC code.

## Testing and verification

- Every step that touches `kernel/irq` or the x86 entry: `ours-verify` on both images, and the kernel-shell tests that hold the layer's races. None of them is in the boot gate; run them on the dbg image with `python3 scripts/kshell_run.py --out out/<dir> irqchip.test irq.test interruptline.test pinlines.test irqdomain.test irqrcu.test irqrevoke.test irqregistry.test`, and `interrupt.test` for steps 4 and 9, which change the state machine it tests.
- New cases go in an arch-neutral file under `kernel/commands`, like `irq_domain_test.cpp`, which includes no x86 header. On `origin/feat/riscv`, `irq_test.cpp` and `interrupt_line_test.cpp` have moved to `kernel/arch/x86/commands` (refactor R25, `3135d44a`), and `pin_lines_test.cpp`, which includes x86 headers, will have to move there too after the merge. Step 5's edit to `interrupt_line_test.cpp` then goes in `kernel/arch/x86/commands/interrupt_line_test.cpp` there.
- Step 4 tested the signal through `interrupt.test`: at each moment of a virtual interrupt's life that one thread can reach, a wait that acks and parks on a second thread, the port path with an observer, and triggers racing a taker's waits and acks, where Idle must be up once the taker has parked; and through the user shell, the same through the system calls and a driver's loop on one port, including a handle closed without an ack. Not tested: that the signal is set again when the debt moved while it was being set (a mutation that removes the loop survives, even at 3000 rounds).
- Cannot be verified here: arm and riscv (nothing builds them in this tree's gate), a PLIC or a GIC on silicon, the mask-during-claim ordering, and any interrupt from a real GPIO.

## Open questions

- The riscv plan targets the PLIC (step 5) and SBI (step 10), and its verify boot is `-machine virt` with no AIA option; that QEMU's default there is the PLIC is from memory. Is an APLIC, which no riscv document plans, in scope for this design? D7 depends on it.
- Whose is the flattening of numbers for a device-tree platform with two or more root controllers: a rule published by the kernel, or one the route service makes up?
- Does the device tree handed to user space need its `/chosen` node scrubbed (boot arguments, random seeds)? From memory, unchecked.
- What shape does the MSI call take: the message returned to the caller, or written by the kernel into a VMO? It needs a device identity for an ITS.
- GICv5 packs the interrupt type into the high bits of the number, which breaks any array indexed by hardware number. Refused as NotSupported in the first arm cut?
- Does a Virtual interrupt's `interrupt_ack` still require a bound port (the wait is the ack when unbound)? Step 4 raises Idle on both paths and leaves the rule as it was.
- Who replaces the interrupt for a pin whose client left without acking: the Driver Manager (`.agents/tasks/driver-manager/DESIGN.md` does not design it), or the controller's driver alone? Until it is settled the pin stays masked.
